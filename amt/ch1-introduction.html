Chapter 01

First Principles

There isn't a singular, most perfect strategy in trading. The path to mastery requires also finding mastery in oneself. Neither gets far without the other, and most approach this journey spectacularly inefficiently.

The Long Way Around

The path to a functioning trading system is not linear. In the early years, it is common to cycle through moving average crossovers, volatility indicators, hand-drawn support and resistance, ICT concepts, SMC theories, and every proprietary methodology in between, all while arriving at the same conclusion: approximately 80% of what circulates as trading knowledge is noise. The 20% that works for a given trader is what matters most, and identifying that 20% generally requires experiencing the 80% firsthand. That is the unglamorous part that the content-to-course pipeline would prefer you not think about.

Many of the frameworks that circulate under branded names are repackaged expressions of concepts that have existed under simpler, more accurate language for decades: imbalances, volume, and inflection points. The mechanism underneath all of them is the same one markets have run on since organized exchanges first existed: an ongoing, fluid interaction between buyers and sellers operating as a continuous auction. This is Auction Market Theory, and it is the sole conceptual foundation of this system.

The exploration phase, as grueling and occasionally enraging as it is, is not wasted time. It is the process by which genuine understanding is earned rather than borrowed. A system built on lived experience compounds differently than one built on borrowed concepts.

This Playbook Was Not Invented. It Was Assembled.

Nothing in the chapters that follow is presented as a new discovery. Auction theory, volume analysis, and the basic mechanics of how buyers and sellers negotiate price have been understood, in one form or another, for as long as organized markets have existed. What this playbook claims is narrower and more honest: it is an attempt to organize, modernize, and adapt principles that have already proven durable, into a framework a discretionary trader can actually use session after session.

That assembly process happened in public, through years spent reading, watching, arguing with, and learning from other traders and educators. WallStSavage, LeoTheTiger, XYZeetrades, and Carmine Rosato all shaped pieces of how this system reads volume, frames sessions, and thinks about risk, alongside countless threads, streams, and conversations whose specific origins have long since blurred into the framework itself. None of them are cited here as authorities to be name-dropped for credibility. They are acknowledged because pretending this system sprang fully formed from first principles would be dishonest, and dishonesty about where ideas come from tends to produce frameworks that collapse the moment reality disagrees with them.

The goal was never to claim ownership over concepts that belong to nobody. The goal was to take ideas that have circulated in various forms for decades, in some cases longer, and organize them into something coherent enough to trade from. If parts of this playbook feel familiar, that is the point. Good ideas are supposed to keep circulating. This is one version of that circulation, written down.

The market is not a conspiracy. It is not controlled by a singular algorithm targeting your stop. It is a large, decentralized collection of buyers and sellers functioning as a continuous auction. Understanding this eliminates a remarkable amount of unnecessary complexity and several expensive bad habits along with it.

The Core Idea: Auction Market Theory

Auction Market Theory holds that markets exist to facilitate trade by continuously testing prices until both buyers and sellers find a level where they are willing to transact. When that level is found, the market stays there. When it isn't found, the market moves until one is located. Two structural states result.

Balance

Supply and demand are in equilibrium. The market rotates between the edges of an accepted range. Neither side has the conviction or inventory to force a break. This is the market's default, comfortable state: both sides are happy to do business here, thank you very much.

Imbalance

One side gains a structural advantage. Price moves directionally until the opposing side re-enters in sufficient size to create a new equilibrium. Imbalance is temporary. Balance is always the destination.

Every analytical decision in this system flows from these two states. The central question driving every session is not where price is going. It is which side currently holds the structural advantage and whether that advantage is growing or deteriorating.

Sessions Are Built Around the Open, Not the Whole Day

This system is built around a specific constraint: sessions are bounded and front-loaded. A typical session runs roughly two to three hours, and the large majority of meaningful decisions get made in a much narrower window than that. The 30-minute opening range and the one-hour initial balance are where the market does most of its early talking. By the time those windows close, the session has usually already told you a great deal about who is in control and what the rest of the day is likely to look like.

That constraint is not a lifestyle preference. It is a performance specification. Cognitive sharpness depletes. The quality of decisions made three hours into a session is measurably worse than those made in the first thirty minutes after the open. A session that extends well past the point of clear thinking, chasing setups long after the opening range and initial balance have already delivered their information, is not discipline. It is a very slow, expensive way to prove a point to nobody in particular.

Two Ideas Worth Sitting With Before Anything Else

  • Match system to personalityThere is no universally correct strategy. The operative question is not "is this a good system?" but "does this system match how I actually behave under pressure?" Trading against your genuine nature produces friction that eventually breaks the system.
  • Markets are not personalA given session's outcome is not a referendum on who you are. The moment a win or a loss becomes a personal inflection point, something that validates or threatens your identity, the quality of every decision that follows degrades. Detaching self-worth from individual outcomes is not detachment from the work. It is what makes the work sustainable.

The Four Principles That Govern Everything That Follows

  • FractalityThe same auction dynamics operate across all timeframes. A reversal on a one-minute chart follows the same structural logic as one on a daily chart. Analysis flows top-down, from macro context to executional precision.
  • ConfluenceNo single input produces a trade. High-probability setups emerge when multiple independent layers (structure, volume, session context, behavior) point to the same conclusion at the same time.
  • Behavioral ReadingMarkets are complex adaptive systems, and the randomness, disorder, and noise inside them are not obstacles to be eliminated. They are features of the system, and they are where opportunity lives. All trade decisions are grounded in three observable behaviors emerging out of that noise: exhaustion (the dominant side runs out of fuel), absorption (the passive side successfully holds a level), and re-engagement (the dominant side returns after a pause). The goal is not to remove uncertainty. It is to understand it well enough to act inside it.
  • Process Over OutcomeIndividual trade results are statistically noisy. A well-executed loss and a poorly-executed win carry opposite information about edge. Only process consistency compounds over time.

One Honest Acknowledgment Before Chapter Two

We never truly know what every other participant in a market is doing. Every read of order flow, every interpretation of a volume profile, every assumption about who is positioned where, is an inference built from incomplete information. That is not a flaw in this framework. It is a description of what trading actually is: the process of making educated assumptions from observable evidence, refined continuously as new evidence arrives, never the discovery of some hidden objective truth about what "the market" wants.

Holding that lightly, rather than pretending certainty exists where it doesn't, is itself a skill. Everything in the chapters ahead is built on observable evidence and the behaviors it tends to produce. None of it is a claim to know what cannot be known.
